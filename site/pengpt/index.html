PenGPT: a generative transformer for handwriting and sketches, explained

PenGPT is a November 2026 project by Sam Greydanus: small transformers that write and sketch from text. A 6M-parameter model writes print and cursive and picks up the handwriting of someone it has never seen from a few of their words; a 54M-parameter model draws from a typed prompt. Greydanus frames the effort as 80% data, 15% speed and 5% architecture, his first project that follows Rich Sutton's Bitter Lesson.

The recipe

PieceWhat it is
Pen strokes as symbolsPen points snap to a grid, so a word becomes a walk in eight directions plus pen-down and pen-up: ten symbols in total. The same tokenizer handles handwriting and drawings.
TokensByte-pair encoding merges common runs of moves into tokens. The post's example word goes from 156 moves to 47 tokens, and sequences end up 3.2× shorter.
ModelA plain decoder-only transformer that cross-attends to what it is told: the text to write, for handwriting, or a SigLIP 2 vector, so a typed phrase can stand in for a picture, for drawings.
Sizes6M parameters for handwriting, 54M for drawing. Training took about 8 minutes and about 35 minutes on an 8×A100 node.

The data

  • Handwriting: 495,590 words by 1,255 writers, mostly from two research collections plus old letters and diaries from public archives. A vision-language model finds legible pages and transcribes them, the pages get cut into words, InkSight traces each word back into strokes, and a set of checks throws out bad traces. Between a third and a half of the words survive.
  • Drawings: 477,670 sketches from ten public datasets; of Quick, Draw!'s 50 million, the best 0.5% were kept.
  • The datasets are not released; the paper's appendix explains how to build them.

What the ablations say

The post compares PenGPT with older approaches, then changes one thing at a time and retrains. Score is nats per pen move on held-out data (lower is better); style rank measures how well the model tells whose hand it is continuing (1 is perfect, 8.5 is chance); legible is how often an OCR model reads a sampled word exactly.

Change one thingEffect the post reports
A tenth of the handwriting dataScore worsens from 0.716 by +0.050 and style rank slips from 1.9 to 3.0. Data is the big row.
No augmentation (drawings)The drawing model stops following its prompts: matches-caption falls from 43% to 6%.
One word per sequence (handwriting)Style rank collapses from 1.9 to 6.0: the model can no longer pick up a hand.
No pen positionNothing for handwriting (+0.001); a small cost on drawings.
No BPE mergesScore slightly better (−0.010): the merges buy speed (3.2× shorter sequences), not quality.
60× the vocabulary, or twice the parametersNo meaningful change either way.
A Graves-style LSTM on the same tokensOnly a bit behind on handwriting and still picks up new hands; on drawings, where sequences are several times longer, it falls well behind.

Picking up a new hand

  • Each training sequence holds up to four words by one writer, which teaches the model that later words look like earlier ones. At sampling time you hand it a few real words and it keeps going in that hand: no style encoder, no fine-tuning.
  • It also works from a photo: InkSight traces a few of the photo's words, and one new writer vector is fit to the traces with the rest of the model frozen. The post shows this for a Sara Teasdale letter and a 1989 NIST form.

Reading the letters

  • One cross-attention head in layer 4 puts 81% of its attention on the letter being written; other heads lock onto the next letter or both. Silencing the "this letter" head costs more than any other.
  • The same head reads a cursive t twice: once for the stem and again for the cross, which in cursive comes only after the word is finished. No head specializes in cursive or print; every head reads the same positions in both.

Captions are not optional

  • Since SigLIP puts pictures and text in the same space, the post asks whether a drawing model could train on picture vectors alone. It cannot: the picture-only model has the best likelihood of the three compared and draws scribbles.
  • The reason the post gives is geometric: SigLIP's vectors for sketches sit in a narrow cone, nearly at right angles to its vectors for text, so a model that has only seen the cone is lost when handed a sentence.
  • Labels are enough to draw labels (78%) but only captions get phrases (43% against 10%).

What it cannot do

  • The drawing model blends but does not bind: a teapot with a face works, but who wears the hat does not, and "facing left" gives the same drawing as "facing right" because the two caption vectors have a cosine similarity of 0.999.
  • Sampled handwriting is less legible than the real thing: 54% against 66% by OCR.
  • Most handwriting was traced from scans, so stroke order is a model's guess, not a record.

Our take, not the author's. We keep this note because the project is a clean data point for small, task-specific models: 6M and 54M parameters, trained in minutes, where the wins came from data volume, cleaning and augmentation rather than architecture. That is the same direction as our Models so far note. The ablation table above is also a useful checklist for any stroke- or sequence-modeling work: check data scale, augmentation and context length before touching the architecture.
