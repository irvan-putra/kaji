Five conflict modes: the Thomas-Kilmann model, explained

The model sorts responses to conflict into five modes using two questions: how much you try to satisfy your own concerns (assertiveness) and how much you try to satisfy the other person's (cooperativeness).

ModeAssertiveCooperativeIn short
CompetinghighlowPursue your own concerns, using whatever power fits.
CollaboratinghighhighWork with the other person toward a solution that fully satisfies both.
CompromisingmiddlemiddleFind a quick, mutually acceptable middle that partly satisfies both.
AvoidinglowlowSidestep, postpone or withdraw without addressing it now.
AccommodatinglowhighYield to the other person's concerns, with an element of self-sacrifice.

What the sources say

  • The five-mode scheme comes from Blake and Mouton (1964) and was reinterpreted by Kenneth Thomas along the two dimensions above (Thomas and Kilmann, 1974).
  • The instrument itself is a commercial 30-item questionnaire sold through The Myers-Briggs Company. Sanggup teaches only the five modes and writes its own scenarios.
  • The publisher describes no mode as better than the others; its statement pairs are matched for desirability.

Our judgment, not a finding. The when-to-use hints in Sanggup's lessons (for example, avoiding only as a pause with a return time) are our own teaching judgment, not claims from the publisher. Use them as starting points for practice.
