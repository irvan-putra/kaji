Is AI safe? Yes, but: an IDSW keynote, checked

Talk reading, 22 Nov 2026. Session: Pesan Systems Engineering Modern dalam Membangun Purwarupa AI ke Sistem Produksi yang Aman, Teruji, dan Layak Dipercaya (roughly: modern systems-engineering lessons for turning AI prototypes into safe, tested, trustworthy production systems), the IDSW 2026 day-two keynote, 10:05-10:50 on 22 Nov, listed speaker Mansur Maturidi Arief (KFUPM; AI Verification and Validation Lab) on the official agenda. The photographed slides carry no title card, so the attribution rests on the agenda listing, the photos' timing inside that slot, and the content's close fit with the announced title, not on anything shown on stage. This note is based on those photographed slides shared with us while the talk ran; the photos are not reproduced here. References and statistics were checked on 22 Nov 2026 against the linked originals.

The talk's arc: build a cat-and-dog classifier the textbook way; watch it fail on a cartoon cat and a capybara; explain the failure with shortcut learning; escalate from accidental failure to deliberate manipulation, from pixel noise to stickers on a stop sign to single words in a sentence; turn from attacks to bias, including a US criminal-justice example and Indonesian-context statistics; and close on the thesis of the title slide: AI can be safe, but only because machine-learning systems are naturally brittle and manipulable, so they need evaluation and monitoring. The structure is sound and its paper trail checks out. The details below keep the scope of each claim attached.

The "yes, but" thesis

The opening slide asked "Is AI safe?" and answered "Yes, but..." with three clauses: machine-learning systems are naturally brittle; they are susceptible to adversarial manipulation; and so they need evaluation and monitoring to ensure safety. Read precisely, the claim is that safety is achievable as an engineering practice, through evaluation before release and monitoring after it, not that models are safe by default. The body of the talk is the evidence for the "but"; the systems-engineering framing in the session's official title is the "yes". That is a defensible thesis, and it matches the practice the references above document: test sets drawn like the training data do not cover difficult cases, shortcuts, manipulation or biased errors, so those need their own checks, and deployment is where the checking starts rather than ends.

The closing intervention slide sorted the defenses into three levers: data augmentation, so training data covers difficult, shifted and perturbed cases rather than only easy ones; model architecture, since model families differ in how they resist specific failure modes; and the train, evaluate and deploy strategy around the model, where drift monitoring, failure review and rollback live. That grouping is conventional ML engineering practice, and it is consistent with the references above: augmentation targets the shortcut and out-of-distribution failures, while the deploy strategy targets what only shows up in production.

Where the practice version lives

The exercises derived from this keynote, classifier lifecycle and out-of-distribution failures, shortcut hunting, adversarial reading, training-bias audits, and an evaluation-and-monitoring plan, are stages in the Coding path of Sanggup, written as paper exercises with the same attributions.
