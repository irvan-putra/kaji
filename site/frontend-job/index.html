The talk's arc: what front-end assembly used to cost, told through the debates teams used to have (frameworks, centering a div, drag-and-drop libraries, state managers, styling, form libraries) and the conventions that kept those choices safe (a typed component contract that rejects a wrong call before it runs); the claim that AI now does most of that assembly; and what remains yours: judgment and taste, living in a design system, trained through three skills, taste, vocabularies and prototyping. The argument

Assembled precisely, the talk's claim is narrower than its title: AI has made assembling interfaces cheap, so the scarce part of front-end work moved to judging them, and that judgment is trainable. His three training grounds: taste, built by looking at curated work until you can tell good from almost-good; vocabularies, because a technique you can name (virtualize, skeleton, optimistic UI, focus ring, ease-out) is a fix you can request, search and review; and prototyping, generating several options cheaply so judgment has something to choose between. His "this detail attention lives in a design system" slide places the accumulated judgment: encoded once, applied everywhere. The closing line, "you have to be good and everyone needs to know you're good", is career advice, and like the title it is his position, not a market measurement.
