# Five conflict modes: the Thomas-Kilmann model, explained

Checked 24 November 2026. Public sources only. No questionnaire was taken or reproduced.

The Thomas-Kilmann model sorts the ways people respond to conflict into five modes. It uses two questions: how much you try to satisfy your own concerns (assertiveness) and how much you try to satisfy the other person's (cooperativeness).

| Mode | Assertive | Cooperative | In short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Competing | high | low | Pursue your own concerns, using whatever power fits. |
| Collaborating | high | high | Work with the other person toward a solution that fully satisfies both. |
| Compromising | middle | middle | Find a quick, mutually acceptable middle that partly satisfies both. |
| Avoiding | low | low | Sidestep, postpone or withdraw without addressing it now. |
| Accommodating | low | high | Yield to the other person's concerns, with an element of self-sacrifice. |

## What the sources say

- The five-mode scheme comes from Blake and Mouton (1964) and was reinterpreted by Kenneth Thomas along the two dimensions above (Thomas and Kilmann, 1974).
- The instrument itself is a commercial 30-item questionnaire sold through The Myers-Briggs Company. Sanggup teaches only the five modes and uses its own scenarios.
- The publisher describes no mode as better than the others. Its statement pairs are matched for desirability.

## Where the "when to use it" guidance comes from

The when-to-use hints in Sanggup's lessons (for example, avoiding as a pause with a return time) are our own teaching judgment, not claims from the publisher. Treat them as starting points for practice, not research findings.
